    Junk, or heritage?

    We are going back. The Artemis programme plans to land crews near the Moon’s south pole, and private landers are already arriving. New engines kick up dust that travels for kilometres and can sandblast whatever it reaches.

    Almost nothing protects these sites. By treaty the hardware still belongs to the country that sent it, but the ground it stands on belongs to no one. The United States asks its partners to keep their distance from the Apollo sites. There is no binding international rule.

    Some of the rubbish is science. The bleached flags, the waste bags, the cut-open Surveyor: each is a fifty-year experiment in what space does to the things we make. It only works if nobody disturbs it.

    It was equipment when it left Earth, and litter when it was dropped. What should we call it now, and who gets to decide?

    About this station

    Every object here is real, and so is every coordinate, mass, date and quotation. The console is the invention. Channel numbers are an index, not radio frequencies. Most of these objects never transmitted anything; some did and stopped; one was never heard from at all.

    The waiting is not invented. Moon signals take the true one-way light time, 1.28 seconds. Mars signals show today’s true one-way light time, calculated from the two planets’ orbits, and compress it to a few seconds. The retroreflector return takes 2.56 seconds because that is how long light takes to go there and back.
